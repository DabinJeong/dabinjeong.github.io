"**AI has broken the system that makes science science!**"

It is a line I hear more and more often these days. Readers complain that papers are now written by AI and their quality has dropped; authors complain that reviewers hand the review off to AI without even reading the paper. Recently I even came across a fairly aggressive reviewer remark along the lines of "I'm not sure a paper that looks AI-written is even worth reviewing." At conferences, from the slides to the posters, everything looks AI-generated, and some have gone so far as to call it "the breakdown of science" [1]. Truth be told, I share the frustration to a degree. Over the past few months, serving as a reviewer at ML conferences, I have been genuinely startled by submissions whose arguments are thin but whose packaging is slick. At the same time, I get a lot of help from AI in my own research every day, so it leaves me with mixed feelings.

I think it has already become hard, even inside academia, to tell AI-generated work apart from human work. If I had to pin it down, AI writing is hard to distinguish from a text written by "a scientist who talks too much, is overconfident in their own arguments, and uses unfamiliar vocabulary." But how much talking is *too* much? How much confidence counts as *over*confidence, and how different does a phrase have to be before it becomes *unfamiliar*? In a paper I read not long ago, I came across the line: "A living organism is like a grand orchestra; just as the violin and the piano play together in concert, proteins cooperate with one another in harmony." The academic culture I was mostly trained in is quite pragmatic and tends to see rhetorical flourishes as excessive, so papers there are written in a concise and plain style. So I was certain this phrase had been generated by AI. But who knows! Expressions that feel so romantic to me that they seem foreign might, in some other culture, be a perfectly fitting and easy-to-grasp analogy for a paper. It is worth asking ourselves whether, in the moments when we don't even want to make the effort to read someone's writing and the context behind it, we are using AI as a convenient excuse.

As a non-native English speaker, I am not free from this problem either. I know my own phrasing can read as foreign and become a target of suspicion; at the same time, I think I understand why people have grown wary of such writing, and it leaves me conflicted. Moments like this bring to mind a line from a poem I love.

> *If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,*<br>
> *But make allowance for their doubting too*
>
> Rudyard Kipling, "If"

It is a shame to watch us meet an unfamiliar phrase, decide "this must have been written by AI," and give up on understanding the thought inside it. Yet I can't simply blame that suspicion either, because I fully understand how exhausting it is for scientists to read and judge the endless flood of plausible-sounding AI slop.

One thing that stayed with me from recent reading is the observation that academia has fewer "**generous supporters**" in the AI era [2]. AI-written reviews follow the standardized evaluation criteria of major conferences so faithfully that they are remarkably good at catching every weakness in a paper, small and large. What has grown rarer is the reviewer who backs a paper out of a wish to cheer it on: not because it is technically flawless or beautifully written, but because the idea inside is interesting. When I look back now at the papers I wrote during my PhD, they feel endlessly rough; and yet, thanks to reviewers who essentially said "it's rough, but I'd like to make it better together through the review," those papers went through the review cycle, got published, I graduated, and I grew another inch in the process. These days, though, that kind of generosity seems to be slowly fading. Perhaps the biggest change AI has brought to science is not the fact that papers have become easier to write. In an environment overflowing with plausible, polished output, could it be that we are growing less generous in recognizing an unfinished idea and extending goodwill to its potential?

What we really need may be a kind of "**armed goodwill**": doubting enough not to be easily taken in, while never letting that doubt blind us to the potential of an idea that is not yet finished. And when we critique someone else's claim, leaving open the possibility that there is something inside it that simply hasn't been polished yet.

P.S. A small aside: what I personally miss most because of AI is that it has taken away my favorite piece of punctuation, the em dash. I loved it because it sets a phrase apart visually while letting you slip it right into the middle of a sentence. These days I hardly use it, just in case my writing gets mistaken for AI's. It makes me a little sad.
