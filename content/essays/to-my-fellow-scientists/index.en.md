"AI has broken the system that makes science science!"

It is a line I hear more and more often these days. The papers are written by AI, and the reviewers complain that paper quality has dropped. At conferences, from the slides to the posters, everything looks AI-generated; some have gone so far as to call it the breakdown of science. I share the frustration to a degree. Over the past few months, serving as a reviewer at ML conferences, I have been genuinely startled by submissions whose arguments are thin but whose packaging is slick. At the same time, I lean on AI for research help every day myself. It leaves me with mixed feelings.

I think it has already become hard, even inside academia, to tell AI-generated work apart from human work. If I had to describe it, AI writing reads a lot like a text written by "a scientist who talks too much, is overconfident in their own arguments, and uses unfamiliar vocabulary." As a non-native English speaker, I know my own phrasing can read as foreign and attract suspicion; at the same time, I think I understand why people have grown wary of such writing. Moments like this bring to mind a line from a poem I love.

> *If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,*<br>
> *But make allowance for their doubting too*
>
> Rudyard Kipling, "If"

It is a shame to watch us meet an unfamiliar phrase and immediately conclude, "this must be AI," then give up on understanding the thought underneath. But I can't blame them either. Having to read and judge piles of plausible-sounding AI slop is exhausting.

One thing that stayed with me from recent reading is the observation that academia has fewer "generous supporters" than it used to [1]. The practice of backing a paper not because it is technically flawless or beautifully written, but because the idea inside is interesting enough to root for, has grown rarer. When I look back now at the papers I wrote in my PhD, they feel endlessly rough; and yet, thanks to reviewers who essentially said "it is rough, but let us work together through the revision cycle to make it better," those papers went through the cycle, got published, and I grew another inch in the process. Perhaps the biggest change AI has brought to science is not that papers have become easier to write. In a landscape overrun with polished-looking output, perhaps what we are losing is the willingness to notice a rough idea and extend it the benefit of the doubt.

What we may really need is a kind of "armed goodwill": doubting enough not to be easily taken in, but not so much that the doubt covers over our ability to see the potential in an idea that is still rough. When we critique someone else's claim, leaving open the possibility that there is something in it that has not yet been fully worked out.
